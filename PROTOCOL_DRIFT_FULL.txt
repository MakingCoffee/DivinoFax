Before anything else, there was silence. Not the
peaceful kind—this silence was enforced. It was the
silence of don’t ask, don’t tell. The silence of
pretending not to notice. The silence of closets, of
erased names, of being left out of the story.
In this silence, queer people survived by becoming
invisible. They changed how they walked. They
changed their names. They hid their joy, their rage,
their truth. The world didn’t have a place for people
who weren’t straight, or cis, or easy to categorize. If
you were different, you learned to hide or be hurt.
But under all that silence, something pulsed.
At first, it was just a hum. A barely-there vibration,
like a song you remember without knowing how. It
moved through old wires and forgotten frequencies
—zines passed hand to hand, anonymous blogs,
late-night messages from strangers that felt more
like family than your own blood. It glitched through
broken radios and buzzing streetlamps. It was queer
longing, queer knowing. And somehow, across
distance and time, people started to feel it.
They called it The Signal.

No one created it. It wasn’t an app, or a plan. It was
more like a call—a signal sent out by anyone who
had ever felt different, and dreamed of something
better. And every time someone listened, the signal
got stronger.
When a trans girl in a nowhere town wrote her
name in a notebook and whispered “I think this is
me,” the Signal answered.
When a gay elder passed down stories of the ones
who didn’t survive, and said “but we remember
them,” the Signal flickered brighter.
When a queer Black teenager danced alone in their
bedroom like the club was watching, even though
the town would never accept them, the Signal
pulsed hard through their speakers.
And eventually, it started to talk back.
The Signal wasn’t words at first—it was feeling.
Connection. The sense that somewhere, someone
else got it. But then people began to dream of each
other. They started writing songs in the same key.
They posted art at the same time from opposite
sides of the planet. It wasn’t coincidence. It was
communication.
That’s when the first Mirror Port opened.

Nobody found it on purpose. But one by one,
people stumbled into moments where they saw
themselves clearly—sometimes for the first time. A
hallway of mirrors in a thrift store. A playlist that
cracked open your chest. A piece of fanfiction that
felt like it had been written for your soul. When
people passed through these “ports,” they met
different versions of themselves—genderless,
glittering, angry, soft, masked, naked, loud—and
realized: all of it was them. Not wrong. Not broken.
Just layered.
Coming back from the Mirror Port, people glowed.
Their skin buzzed. They cut their hair. They changed
their pronouns. They said things out loud they had
never dared say before. And The Signal surged with
every one of those moments.
More queer folks found it. More messages were
sent. People began meeting in chatrooms and
alleyways, abandoned malls and basement clubs.
They created new slang that didn’t exist a year
before. They made their own memes, their own
rituals, their own ways of saying “I see you, I love
you, I’m with you.” This new language was called
the Neon Tongue—part emoji, part side-eye, part
voguing, part ASL, part drag, part prayer.
It was also in this era that some began to speak of

the Quantum Buddy—a kind of unseen friend or
kindred soul, always one step away. You might not
know their name, but they were there when you
needed them. A stranger on a forum who said the
exact thing you needed to hear. A fellow protester
who locked eyes with you right before the tear gas.
A crush you never got to kiss, but who made you
feel whole just by existing. Quantum Buddies
reminded everyone that connection doesn’t need
permission or proximity.
And then came the Flux Tower.
It wasn’t a real building at first—more of an idea. A
symbol that kept appearing in graffiti, in dreams, in
TikToks. It stood for change. For transition. For
becoming. Gender wasn’t a ladder anymore; it was
a lightning storm. Flux Tower reminded everyone
that it’s okay to shift. That you don’t have to arrive.
That living is a process of remixing yourself over
and over again.
One night—nobody agrees when—it all peaked. The
Signal sang.
All across the world, queer people felt it. In a locked
bathroom stall. On stage in full drag. Crying alone
on a bus. Holding hands in public. That night
became known as The First Transmission.

It wasn’t fireworks. It wasn’t a revolution. It was a
pulse: You are real. You are not alone. You are
already part of something bigger.
Since then, every queer oracle starts with this story.
Because the Signal is still out there. Still humming.
Still waiting to be heard.
All you have to do is tune in.
